Beyond Trade-offs: A Unified Framework for
Privacy, Robustness, and Communication Efficiency
in Federated Learning

arXiv:2508.12978v2 [cs.LG] 25 Dec 2025

Yue Xia, Student Member, IEEE, Tayyebeh Jahani-Nezhad, Member, IEEE, Rawad Bitar, Member, IEEE

Abstract—We propose F E D -DPR O C, a novel federated learning
framework designed to jointly provide differential privacy (DP),
Byzantine robustness, and communication efficiency. Central to
our approach is the concept of robust-compatible compression,
which allows reducing the bi-directional communication overhead
without undermining the robustness of the aggregation. We
instantiate our framework as R O B AJ O L, which integrates the
Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL)-based compression mechanism with
robust averaging for robustness. Our theoretical analysis establishes the compatibility of JL transform with robust averaging,
ensuring that R O B AJ O L maintains robustness guarantees, satisfies
DP, and substantially reduces communication overhead. We
further present simulation results on CIFAR-10, Fashion MNIST,
and FEMNIST, validating our theoretical claims. We compare
R O B AJ O L with a state-of-the-art communication-efficient and
robust FL scheme augmented with DP for a fair comparison,
demonstrating that R O B AJ O L outperforms existing methods in
terms of robustness and utility under different Byzantine attacks.
Index Terms—federated learning, Byzantine robustness, communication efficiency, differential privacy

I. I NTRODUCTION
Federated learning (FL) [2] enables training machine learning models over distributed, sensitive data held by multiple
clients. The federator (or server) iteratively broadcasts a global
model, collects locally computed updates from clients, and
aggregates them to refine the model. While raw data remain
local, this iterative process introduces several fundamental
challenges: (a) privacy: local updates can leak information
about the clients’ data, e.g., through gradient inversion attacks [3], [4]; (b) robustness: if care is not taken, malicious
(Byzantine) clients may disrupt the training process by manipulating their updates [5]; and (c) communication efficiency:
frequent exchange of high-dimensional model updates between
the federator and the clients imposes a significant communication cost [6], [7]. A practical FL framework should address
all three challenges simultaneously [6]–[9].
Since the federator may be honest-but-curious, privacypreserving FL schemes can be split into two large categories

depending on what the federator is allowed to see: (a) using
secure aggregation, or (b) using differential privacy (DP)
mechanisms. Secure aggregation techniques ensure that the
federator observes only an aggregation of the client updates,
which is essential for the learning process to proceed [10]–
[13]. DP-based approaches instead perturb the updates with
noise [14] so that the semi-honest fedeartor observes either
individual noisy updates, or a noisy version of the aggregated
updates [15], i.e., using secure aggregation on top of DP. Although secure aggregation avoids explicit noise insertion and
thus does not incur any utility loss as with DP mechanisms, it
creates high communication overhead and remains vulnerable
to membership inference attacks [16].
Robustness against malicious clients is typically achieved
through robust aggregation rules such as Krum [5], coordinatewise trimmed mean and median [17], Bulyan [18], and
ProDiGy [19]. These aggregation rules detect and downweight
or remove outlier updates based on certain statistical or
geometric metrics. Other defenses, such as [20], [21], assign
scores to clients or their updates before aggregating them;
however, they require the federator to have an auxiliary clean
dataset representative of clients’ data.
Communication efficiency is typically addressed through
compression methods including quantization, sparsification,
and distillation [7], [22]–[28], which either reduce the dimension of the model updates or represent them using fewer bits.
Many works address pairs of these challenges. For instance,
privacy and robustness are jointly considered in [12], [13],
[20], [29]–[34]; communication efficiency and privacy are
studied in [15], [35]–[37]; and communication efficiency and
robustness are explored in [38]–[41]. Among these, [40]
proposed Byz-EF21 and Byz-EF21-BC, which utilize biased
compressors, e.g., top-k sparsification, to improve uplink
communication efficiency (i.e., reducing the size of clientto-federator communication cost) and bidirectional communication efficiency (i.e., reducing both client-to-federator and
federator-to-client communication cost), respectively. Their
approach carefully structures model updates so that, when robust aggregation rules are applied, the algorithm still converges
in the presence of malicious clients.
Despite these advances, addressing all three challenges
simultaneously remains underexplored. The approaches [42]–
[44] use sign-based compressors to achieve both robustness
and communication efficiency. These sign-based compressors
are also differentially private, thereby offering a degree of
privacy protection. Similarly, [45] uses a lattice quantizer with

2

randomized response, and [46] applies a differentially private ternary-based compressor with majority voting. Although
these methods unify the three objectives, they use aggressive
compression mechanisms and lack proven robust aggregation
rules, resulting in weaker robustness guarantees.
In [47], the authors employ top-k sparsification, momentumdriven variance reduction, and DP. However, no convergence
proof is provided, nor are the proven robust aggregation rules
employed. In [48], the robust aggregation rule FLTrust [21] is
integrated with compressive sensing. While the authors claim
the scheme also preserves privacy, no formal privacy proof is
given. Moreover, the approach assumes homogeneous client
data, which is not realistic in FL scenarios.
In this paper, we propose F E D -DPR O C, a unified framework
that simultaneously ensures provable DP and robustness guarantees, and offers flexible communication compression. The
core idea is the introduction of robust-compatible compression, i.e., compression methods that preserve the robustness
guarantees of arbitrary robust aggregation rules.
In each iteration of F E D -DPR O C, clients compute gradients
based on the current global model, perturb them with DP noise,
and apply a momentum-based update to strengthen robustness
against malicious attacks [49]. The clients then apply a robustcompatible compression mechanism and send the compressed
updates to the federator. The federator aggregates the updates using any robust aggregation protocol and obtains a
compressed aggregation. To further reduce the communication
cost, the federator delegates the decompression task to the
clients. That is, the federator does not decompress the aggregation; instead, it broadcasts the compressed aggregation directly
to the clients. The clients then decompress the aggregation,
update the global model, and proceed to the next iteration.
To instantiate F E D -DPR O C, we propose R O B AJ O L, which
employs Gaussian noise as a DP mechanism, and the JohnsonLindenstrauss (JL) transform [50] as a compression method.
We theoretically prove that the JL transform satisfies the
robust-compatible property under the robust averaging criterion given in [9], i.e., the robustness guarantee that a robust
aggregation holds in the original vector space is preserved in
the compressed vector space resulting from the JL transform.
We further show that R O B AJ O L ensures formal DP guarantees
and substantially reduces bidirectional communication overhead while maintaining robustness.
We provide extensive empirical evaluations validating the
theoretical guarantees of R O B AJ O L across a range of compression rates, Byzantine attacks, and DP levels, using multiple
benchmark datasets. We compare R O B AJ O L with Byz-EF21BC [40], to which we additionally incorporate DP for fair
comparison. Byz-EF21-BC makes the non-robust-compatible
compressor top-k sparsification “compatible” with robust aggregation by modifying the clients’ model updates. Both
schemes reduce bidirectional communication costs; however,
our results show that R O B AJ O L achieves superior robustness
and utility across a wide range of malicious attacks.

F E D -DPR O C introduces the concept of robust-compatible
compression (Fig. 2) that allows us to integrate compression
into robust aggregation while preserving robustness.
Definition 4 (Robust-compatible Compression). Let AGG :
(Rd )n → Rd be a robust aggregation rule that takes as input
n vectors in Rd , out of which b < n/2 can be arbitrarily
manipulated, and outputs an aggregation in Rd that is faithful
to the average of the n − b benign vectors; where the faithfulness is measured according to a desired robustness criterion.
Let C OMPRESS : Rd → Rk , with k ≪ d, be a compression
mechanism that takes a vector in Rd and compresses it to
a vector in Rk , and let D ECOMPRESS : Rk → Rd be the
corresponding decompression rule.
C OMPRESS is said to be robust-compatible according to
the desired robustness criterion if the following holds: the
composition C OMPRESS ◦AGG ◦D ECOMPRESS remains robust
according to the desired robustness criterion. In other words,
when AGG is applied on the compressed version of the n
vectors (now in Rk ), it outputs an average vector (also in
Rk ) that, after decompression to Rd , remains faithful to the
average of the n − b benign original vectors.
In FL, such compatibility ensures that the system remains
robust to adversarial behavior even after dimensionality reduction, while significantly reducing communication costs.

In this section, we present R O B AJ O L, one instantiation of
F E D -DPR O C with a specific noise mechanism, robust aggregation, and compression scheme. The key idea of R O B AJ O L
lies in its provably robust, private, and communication-efficient
design, achieved by integrating the Johnson–Lindenstrauss
(JL) transform [50] as the compression method and a (b, κ)robust averaging [9] to ensure Byzantine robustness. This
instantiation demonstrates how F E D -DPR O C can be realized
using principled components with theoretical guarantees.
A. Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) Compression
To reduce communication overhead, R O B AJ O L employs
the JL transform as the compression method C OMPRESS. JL
transform is the compression method satisfying JL lemma
with high probability. The JL lemma guarantees that highdimensional vectors can be projected into a lower-dimensional
subspace with only a small distortion in pairwise distances.

Remark 1. Regarding convergence (robustness), a smaller
ϵJL means that (a) the κ′ provided by the composition
C OMPRESS◦AGG◦D ECOMPRESS is closer to the κ guaranteed
by AGG, i.e., the resulting scheme is as robust as AGG; and
(b) the additional error term δRA is smaller. This indicates
a trade-off between communication efficiency and robustness.
A larger compression rate results in a higher ϵJL , which in
turn makes κ′ deviate further from the original κ and yields a
larger δRA . Conversely, a smaller compression rate increases
the communication overhead but allows κ′ to remain closer
to κ, and also leads to a smaller error term δRA .

V. E XPERIMENTS
We evaluate the practical performance of R O B AJ O L by conducting experiments on three datasets: Fashion MNIST [64]
and CIFAR-10 [65], both distributed across n = 15 clients,
and FEMNIST [66] distributed across n = 50 clients.
A. Training Parameters and Data Distribution
For Fashion MNIST, we set minibatch size ϱ = 60, total
global iterations T = 2000, and learning rate η = 0.25. We
train a neural network consisiting of three fully connected
layers (in total 535818 parameters). For CIFAR-10, we set
minibatch size ϱ = 128, total global iterations T = 10000,
and learning rate η (t) = 0.25 for t ≤ 8000, and η (t) = 0.025
afterward. We use a convolutional neural network composed
of two convolutional blocks followed by a linear classifier (in
total 789706 parameters). For FEMNIST, we set minibatch
size ϱ = 128, total global iterations T = 1000, and learning
rate η (t) = 0.2 for t ≤ 800, and η (t) = 0.02 afterward.
We use a convolutional neural network consisting of two
convolutional layers and a two-layer fully connected classifier
(in total 1690046 parameters).
All experiments are conducted with a fixed momentum
coefficient β = 0.9. We use the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)

8

as the activation function and Cross-Entropy as the loss function. In each iteration, clients randomly sample a minibatch
from their local training dataset using subsampling without
replacement and perform local training on the minibatch.
To simulate non-IID data, for Fashion MNIST and CIFAR10, we randomly divide the clients into 10 groups. A training
sample with label j is assigned to group j w.p. a > 0,
and to each of the remaining groups w.p. 1−a
9 . Within each
group, data is uniformly distributed among the clients. In our
experiments, we set a = 0.5 to reflect a heterogeneous setting.
FEMNIST is inherently heterogeneous. FEMNIST consists of
3500 clients with a varying number of samples per client,
with a mean 226.83. We filter the clients having at least 380
samples, and randomly sample n = 50 clients from them.
B. Compression Method
For communication efficiency, we use the Count
Sketch [61], cf. Example 1, to construct the JL transform
matrix R. The number of block matrices is set to be
p = 10. For Fashion MNIST, the compression rate kd = 10.
Compression rates are varied across {10, 30, 50} for CIFAR10, and across {10, 50, 100, 500, 1000, 2000} for FEMNIST.
C. Byzantine Robustness
We assume that 20% of the clients are malicious, i.e.,
b = 3 for Fashion MNIST and CIFAR-10 and b = 10 for
FEMNIST. Since R O B AJ O L supports arbitrary robust aggregation rules satisfying (b, κ)-robust averaging, we test it with
the following rules: Krum [5], Trimmed Mean (TM) [17]
and Median (Med) [17]. These robust aggregations rules are
proved to be (b, κ)-robust averaging [9], [67]. We also use the
Nearest Neighbor Mixing (NNM) [67], where each client’s
update is averaged with those of a subset of its neighbors to
enhance the robustness under heterogeneity. Combining Krum,
TM, or Med with NNM improves the robustness coefficient
κ of the (b, κ)-robust averaging rule [67]. The malicious
clients perform one of the following attacks: Label Flipping
(LF) attack [68], a-little-is-enough (ALIE) attack [69], Sign
Flipping (SF) attack [68], Min-Max attack [70], Min-Sum
attack [70] and FoE attack [71].
D. Differential Privacy
To implement DP, we set the Gaussian noise scale as
σDP = 2C
ϱ × σNM with the clipping threshold C = 2 for
Fashion MNIST, C = 3 for FEMNIST, and C = 4 for CIFAR10, and σNM is the noise multiplier. Since the DP guarantee
(ϵDP , δDP ) is sensitive to the choice of sampling strategy,
e.g., Poisson sampling and sampling with(out) replacement,
we report the noise multiplier σNM instead. We use Opacus [72] to compute the privacy budget (ϵDP , δDP ) via RDP
accounting. Note that Opacus assumes Poisson subsampling,
whereas our simulation uses sampling without replacement
(fixed-size minibatches). Since Poisson subsampling leads to
a looser privacy bound under RDP analysis than fixed-size
sampling [73], the ϵDP reported by Opacus is a conservative
upper bound on the actual privacy loss incurred in our setting.

VI. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we propose F E D -DPR O C, a unified approach
ensuring differential privacy, Byzantine robustness, and communication efficiency. We introduce the concept of robustcompatible compression, instantiated as R O B AJ O L, which uses
the JL transform for compression, robust averaging to guarantee robustness, and the Gaussian DP mechanism to ensure
privacy. Theoretical analysis and experiments show that our
approach preserves robustness and privacy while significantly
reducing communication overhead.
