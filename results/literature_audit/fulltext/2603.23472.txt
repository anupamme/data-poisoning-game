Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) enables heterogeneous clients to collaboratively train a shared
model without centralizing their raw data, offering an inherent level of privacy. However, gradients and model updates can still leak sensitive information, while malicious servers may mount
adversarial attacks such as Byzantine manipulation. These vulnerabilities highlight the need to
address differential privacy (DP) and Byzantine robustness within a unified framework. Existing
approaches, however, often rely on unrealistic assumptions such as bounded gradients, require
auxiliary server-side datasets, or fail to provide convergence guarantees. We address these limitations by proposing Byz-Clip21-SGD2M, a new algorithm that integrates robust aggregation with
double momentum and carefully designed clipping. We prove high-probability convergence guarantees under standard L-smoothness and σ-sub-Gaussian gradient noise assumptions, thereby
relaxing conditions that dominate prior work. Our analysis recovers state-of-the-art convergence
rates in the absence of adversaries and improves utility guarantees under Byzantine and DP settings. Empirical evaluations on CNN and MLP models trained on MNIST further validate the
effectiveness of our approach.

Introduction

The rapid deployment of large-scale machine learning models has positioned Federated Learning
(FL) [Konečnỳ et al., 2016, McMahan et al., 2017a] as a central paradigm, enabling a collection
of potentially heterogeneous clients, ranging from smartphones and sensors to data centers, to
collaboratively train a shared model without transmitting their raw data to a central server [Li
et al., 2020, Yang et al., 2019]. While this setting offers an inherent degree of privacy, it is far from
complete: gradients and model parameters can still leak sensitive information [Zhu et al., 2019,
Geiping et al., 2020], and adversaries may exploit the distributed nature of FL to launch attacks such
as membership inference, model stealing, or Byzantine manipulation [Shokri et al., 2017, Tramèr
et al., 2016, Blanchard et al., 2017]. This dual vulnerability highlights the need to study differential
privacy, to mitigate privacy leakage, and Byzantine robustness, to ensure resilience against arbitrary
or malicious participants, within a unified framework. Both challenges share the common difficulty
of optimizing with corrupted or unreliable updates, whether the corruption arises from deliberate
noise injection for privacy or from adversarial manipulations. Moreover, addressing these issues is
not only of algorithmic importance: modern AI systems must also comply with evolving regulatory
requirements, such as GDPR and the EU AI Act for privacy [GDP, 2016, EUA, 2024], or the EU
NIS2 Directive, Cyber Resilience Act, and NIST AI Risk Management Framework for security and
robustness [NIS, 2022, Cyb, 2024, Tabassi et al., 2023].
In this context, we propose to study Byzantine robustness and differential privacy jointly, with
the goal of establishing a principled foundation for robust, privacy-preserving federated learning
and providing a pathway toward trustworthy AI in practice. A central open challenge is to move
1

beyond the strong assumptions that dominate the current literature. Most theoretical analyses in
differential privacy are carried out under assumptions such as bounded gradients – an assumption
that fails even for simple quadratic objectives [Li et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023, Lowy et al., 2023]
– or by focusing on the full-batch regime [Shulgin et al., 2025b]. Only recently have approaches
emerged that achieve strong privacy guarantees while retaining favorable optimization performance
under milder conditions [Islamov et al., 2025b]. Byzantine robustness has evolved largely as a
separate line of work [Lyu et al., 2022], yet its analyses similarly depend on gradient boundedness
to establish convergence guarantees [Blanchard et al., 2017, Mhamdi et al., 2018]. Attempts to
combine DP with Byzantine resilience remain even more limited: existing methods either assume
access to an auxiliary server-side dataset to obtain a better gradient approximation [Xiang et al.,
2023], rely on unrealistic aggregation rules [Guerraoui et al., 2021b, Zhang and Hu, 2023], impose
restrictive bounded-gradient assumptions [Allouah et al., 2023b].
Motivated by the shortcomings of prior theoretical results, we arrive at the following guiding
question:
Is it possible to develop an algorithm that provably converges in the presence of malicious clients while simultaneously providing strong differential privacy guarantees under
standard assumptions?
Main Contributions. We provide an affirmative answer to the above question. In doing so, we
make the following contributions:
• We introduce Byz-Clip21-SGD2M, a new algorithm that combines robust aggregation with
double momentum and carefully designed clipping, achieving efficient performance under both
Byzantine and differential privacy adversaries.
• We provide a high-probability convergence analysis of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M under L-smoothness
and σ-sub-Gaussian gradient noise, thereby relaxing assumptions that have been used in prior
work (see Table 1 for details). Our results improve utility guarantees by avoiding unrealistic
assumptions, while recovering state-of-the-art convergence rates in the absence of Byzantine
or DP adversaries.
• We complement our theoretical analysis with empirical validation, demonstrating the effectiveness of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M on CNN and MLP models trained on the MNIST dataset.

Related Works

Error Feedback. Biased compression in federated learning reduces communication by transmitting compressed local updates that are not unbiased estimates of the original signal [Ajalloeian and
Stich, 2020, Beznosikov et al., 2023, Demidovich et al., 2023]. Common examples include Top-K
sparsification [Alistarh et al., 2017, Wangni et al., 2018] and sign-based compression [Bernstein
et al., 2018, Stich et al., 2018]. While these methods are efficient in practice, the introduced bias
can accumulate and affect convergence, requiring Error Feedback (EF) [Seide et al., 2014] techniques to mitigate its impact. Earlier works primarily focused on the single-node setting or relied
on restrictive assumptions, such as bounded gradients, bounded compression error, or gradient
dissimilarity, to establish convergence [Stich et al., 2018, Stich and Karimireddy, 2019, Koloskova
et al., 2019]. Moreover, the convergence rates of EF degrade in the presence of client heterogeneity,
and this dependence is intrinsic rather than a proof artifact [Gorbunov et al., 2020]. To overcome
these limitations, [Richtárik et al., 2021] proposed EF21, a variant whose guarantees do not depend
on heterogeneity bounds. Nevertheless, EF21-SGD still requires increasingly large batch sizes to
achieve a target accuracy [Fatkhullin et al., 2024]. Importantly, this limitation is not fundamental:
recent work demonstrates that incorporating Heavy-Ball momentum removes the need for large
batches [Fatkhullin et al., 2024]. Later, EF21 was extended to several practical setups [Fatkhullin
et al., 2025, Makarenko et al., 2022]. In a parallel line of research, Gao et al. [2023, 2025b] introduced EControl-type Error Feedback methods with convergence guarantees extending to the

Byzantine Robust Optimization. Naively averaging updates from clients in a distributed setting lacks robustness since even a single Byzantine client can destabilize training [Chen et al.,
2017]. To address this, numerous alternatives to averaging have been proposed to enhance robustness against adversarial updates [Pillutla et al., 2022, Yin et al., 2018, Damaskinos et al., 2019,
Karimireddy et al., 2021, Allouah et al., 2024a].
Nevertheless, permutation-invariant algorithms, i.e., the ones whose output is unchanged under shuffling of the stochastic gradients computed on different workers [Karimireddy et al., 2021,
Definition B], alone cannot ensure convergence in the Byzantine regime [Karimireddy et al., 2021,
Theorem II]. This limitation can be alleviated by incorporating update shuffling and averaging in
random groups [Karimireddy et al., 2020], variance-reduction techniques [Wu et al., 2020, Gorbunov et al., 2022b], or preprocessing based on the averaging with nearest neighbors [Allouah
et al., 2023a]. Beyond these, other approaches include concentration-based gradient filtering [Alistarh et al., 2018, Allen-Zhu et al., 2020], server-free protocols with random checks [Gorbunov et al.,
2022a], redundant computations [Chen et al., 2018, Rajput et al., 2019], and reputation-based
mechanisms [Rodrıguez-Barroso et al., 2020, Regatti et al., 2020, Xu and Lyu, 2020]. More recent
works further extend these ideas to partial participation [Malinovsky et al., 2024], decentralized
training [He et al., 2022], and communication compression [Rammal et al., 2024]. In most of these
cases, the theoretical analysis is carried out under the L-smoothness and ζ 2 -gradient dissimilarity1

Differentially Private Optimization. Differential privacy (DP) is typically achieved by clipping each client’s update and adding Gaussian noise, limiting the influence of any individual client
McMahan et al. [2017b]. In central DP, a trusted server adds noise before updating the global
model, while in local DP, clients perturb updates before sending them, protecting privacy even
from the server [Kasiviswanathan et al., 2011, Allouah et al., 2024b]. Local DP offers stronger privacy but reduces model utility, though this can be mitigated using secure shufflers or aggregators
[Erlingsson et al., 2019, Bonawitz et al., 2017]. DP can also enable update compression without
additional cost [Chaudhuri et al., 2022, Hegazy et al., 2023]. Private optimization methods, such
as DP-SGD [Abadi et al., 2016], enforce DP by clipping gradients and adding noise scaled to the
clipping sensitivity. However, most convergence analyses neglect the bias introduced by clipping.
For smooth functions, guarantees typically assume either bounded gradient norms [Li et al., 2020,
Zhang et al., 2020b, Murata and Suzuki, 2023, Wang et al., 2023, Lowy et al., 2023, Wang et al.,
2024] or that clipping is effectively inactive [Noble et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2024]. Recently, several
works have provided improved analyses of DP-Clip-SGD and its variants. In the single-node setting,
generalized smoothness has also been considered without restrictive requirements for DP-Clip-SGD
[Koloskova et al., 2023]. Next, Khah et al. [2025] also focus on the single-node setting and provide
high-probability results under the heavy-tailed noise assumption and arbitrary clipping level for
DP-Clip-SGD. Zhao et al. [2025] consider a projected SGD-type method utilizing DP mean estimation and provide its in-expectation convergence bounds. Compagnoni et al. [2026] study SDE
approximation of DP-Clip-SGD in the single-node setup. In the FL setting, analysis under realistic
conditions in the presence of differential privacy has been studied in Islamov et al. [2025b], Shulgin
et al. [2025a,b].
Differentially Private and Byzantine Robust Methods. Although substantial progress has
been made separately in differential privacy and Byzantine robustness, their interaction in distributed learning is still not well understood. Recent efforts to address both aspects at once remain
limited. A key limitation of existing theoretical work is the reliance on restrictive assumptions,
often unrealistic and mainly introduced to simplify the analysis. For example, some studies focus
on a single aggregation rule [Guerraoui et al., 2021b], while others assume a particular noise structure near the minimizer or even access to a non-private external server-side dataset [Xiang et al.,
2023]. Other analyses restrict attention to the strongly convex case [Gao et al., 2024, Allouah
et al., 2023b] or impose functional similarity assumptions that do not hold in heterogeneous federated learning [Lan et al., 2025]. Further limitations include assuming i.i.d. momentum buffers [Gu
et al., 2025] or bounded-gradient conditions [Guerraoui et al., 2021a, Gao et al., 2024, Allouah et al.,
2023b]. Taken together, these limitations highlight the significant challenges in jointly addressing
differential privacy and Byzantine robustness.

for establishing utility bounds in Table 1. Compared to prior work, our theoretical analysis relies
on the weakest set of assumptions.

Algorithm Design

We introduce the Byz-Clip21-SGD2M algorithm, designed to operate under both Byzantine failures
and differential privacy noise. The main steps are outlined in Algorithm 1, followed by a detailed
discussion highlighting the key design innovations and their rationale.
Momentum Mechanism. Byz-Clip21-SGD2M employs two momentum buffers, {vit }i∈G on the
client side and {mti }i∈G on the server side, both essential for mitigating noise during training. On the
client side, momentum with parameter β smooths mini-batch gradient noise; without it, the method
may fail to converge, as seen in algorithms such as EF21-SGD and Clip21-SGD, which provably fail
to converge to arbitrary accuracy ε under mini-batch noise [Fatkhullin et al., 2024, Islamov et al.,
2025b]. On the server side, momentum with parameter β̂ counteracts the accumulation of DP-noise
in the buffers {mti }i∈G , preventing it from destroying convergence due to fast accumulation of the
DP noise in the updates.
Clipping. It is crucial to bound model updates before injecting DP-noise. Without clipping,
updates may have arbitrarily large norms, which would dominate the injected noise and invalidate
the privacy guarantee. Clipping restricts each update to a controlled range, ensuring that the
privacy guarantees remain meaningful and that the added noise achieves its intended effect. In
Byz-Clip21-SGD2M, we employ the standard norm-clipping mechanism, which rescales updates
relative to a threshold τ :

Error Feedback. To mitigate client drift, which arises from the potentially different local data
distributions across clients, we augment our method with an EF21-style error feedback mechanism
on the client side. Error feedback controls the discrepancy between the true global update and
local updates sent to the server. Incorporating this mechanism into Byz-Clip21-SGD2M allows the
algorithm to compensate for the bias introduced by heterogeneous client data, thereby ensuring
convergence under larger levels of data heterogeneity. This design choice makes the method robust
to one of the central difficulties in federated optimization and allows eliminating the need for the
bounded gradient assumption imposed in prior work.
Robust Aggregation. At the heart of robust Byz-Clip21-SGD2M lies the aggregation rule RAgg.
To mitigate adversarial clients that attempt to disrupt training, we employ a robust aggregation
rule satisfying Definition 3.2. Robust aggregation limits the damage that Byzantine clients can
cause during training.
Challenges of Designing Differential Private and Byzantine-Robust Algorithms. Designing algorithms that are both differentially private and Byzantine-robust is inherently challenging
as the two objectives often conflict with each other. Differential privacy requires adding extra noise
to the raw updates, which reduces their consistency and makes it easier for adversarial updates
to remain undetected. Moreover, using robust aggregation to combine the DP-noisy updates can
amplify the detrimental effects of noise, further hindering convergence. Consequently, theoretical
analyses must be carried out with particular care and often under restrictive assumptions. Overcoming these limitations requires carefully designing algorithms that balance privacy, robustness,
and practical learnability.

In Corollary 5.1, the first term corresponds to the error arising from privacy, while the last term
reflects the error introduced by the presence of Byzantine clients. The middle term represents the
additional penalty incurred when enforcing both requirements simultaneously. Our utility bound
takes a similar form as the lower bound in the strongly convex case [Allouah et al., 2023b], which
demonstrates that our analysis is essentially tight. In particular, Theorem 5.1 shows that the
impacts of privacy and Byzantine adversaries multiply. The privacy analysis of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M
then follows from the well-known result in (Dwork et al. [2014, Theorem 3.22] in combination with
the advanced composition theorem ([Dwork et al., 2014, Theorem 3.20]), we obtain that all T
iterations of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M are local (ε, δ)-differentially private.
Comparison to prior work. We analyze Byz-Clip21-SGD2M under the standard L-smoothness
and σ-sub-Gaussian assumptions, thereby improving over earlier work that required the far more
restrictive bounded-gradient condition [Zhu and Ling, 2022, Allouah et al., 2023b]. To our knowledge, the only high-probability study of optimization algorithms in the simultaneous presence of
DP and Byzantine adversaries is due to Xiang et al. [2023]; however, their analysis is limited to
a single attack model and relies on strong assumptions, thereby limiting its applicability. Other
works rely on expectation-based analysis, yet still impose restrictive assumptions; see Table 1 for
a concrete comparison. By contrast, our convergence guarantee for Byz-Clip21-SGD2M depends
only logarithmically on the failure probability α, which is consistent with standard high-probability
analyses, and holds for any aggregation rule satisfying Definition 3.2.
Main Challenges in the Analysis. Server-side aggregation induces a bias between the server
P
aggregate g t and the honest-average update G1 i∈G mti . This bias scales multiplicatively with the
DP-noise variance σω and cδbyz ; controlling it without assuming that the gradients are bounded
requires a careful analysis with well-calibrated hyperparameters. To address this, we treat clipping
as a contractive compressor, with the complication that its contraction factor is input-dependent,
which motivates a high-probability analysis. Using a refined inductive argument, we show that
the inputs to the clipping operator remain bounded with high probability. Finally, obtaining only
poly-logarithmic dependence on the failure probability α hinges on a careful decomposition of the
noise terms, each controlled via concentration inequalities.

Experiments

To demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed algorithm, we test the performance of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M
against Byz-Clip-SGD (Algorithm 3) and Safe-DSHB (Algorithm 4) [Allouah et al., 2023b] when
training CNN and MLP models on the MNIST dataset [LeCun et al., 2010]. We fix the number
of regular clients to 20 and distribute the dataset among them equally. Then, we add Byzantine clients that perform IPM attack [Xie et al., 2020] on the vectors transmitted from the clients
to the server. In particular, each Byzantine client computes the average of the transmitted vectors of regular clients and multiplies the average by −10 as described in Xie et al. [2019] (Figure 1). We test performance when varying the number of Byzantine clients and the privacy budget
ε ∈ {3, 8, 13, 18, 23}. For each algorithm we perform an extensive tuning of the learning rate parameter in {10, 1, 10−1 , 10−2 , 10−3 } and clipping threshold τ ∈ {1, 10−1 , 10−2 , 10−4 , 10−5 , 10−6 }. For
Byz-Clip21-SGD2M and Safe-DSHB, we fix the local momentum parameter β = 0.1, while we use
β̂ = 0.01 for Byz-Clip21-SGD2M. In such a setting, every algorithm allocates an equal amount of
privacy budget for tuning, since the set of tested hyperparameters remains the same across algorithms. We test the performance of algorithms with a batch size 64 for MLP and 32 for CNN
training. Consistent with our theory, where we assume access to σ-sub-Gaussian stochastic gradients, wepdisable privacy amplification by sub-sampling and inject DP noise with standard deviation
σω = τε T log (1/δ), where T is the total number of iterations.
The results in Figure 1 present the mean and one standard deviation across three random
seeds. In all configurations, Byz-Clip21-SGD2M is competitive, matching or surpassing the baselines in test accuracy. These findings align with our theory and provide empirical evidence that
Byz-Clip21-SGD2M is an effective method for training under DP noise injection and Byzantine
attacks. Additional experimental results and training details are summarized in Section F.

7

Conclusion and Limitations

In this work, we present Byz-Clip21-SGD2M, a new algorithm that admits provable convergence
under standard L-smoothness and σ-sub-Gaussian gradient noise in the simultaneous presence of
DP and Byzantine adversaries. Several directions merit further study: (i) establishing lower bounds
without the bounded-gradient assumption of Allouah et al. [2023b]; (ii) tightening our convergence
guarantees to match known lower bounds exactly when DP and Byzantine adversaries are considered separately; (iii) extending the analysis of Byz-Clip21-SGD2M to heavy-tailed gradient noise
and relaxed smoothness assumptions [Zhang et al., 2020a, Alimisis et al., 2025]; (iv) strengthening
the privacy guarantees via amplification by data and client subsampling.

Additional Experiments and Training Details

Our implementation builds on the codebase of Horváth and Richtárik [2020] to simulate a distributed environment, while the attack implementations are adopted from Gorbunov et al. [2022b].
It can be found via the link https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ByzClip21SGD2M.

F.1

Training Details

In our experiments, we compare Byz-Clip21-SGD2M against Byz-Clip-SGD and Safe-DSHB [Allouah
et al., 2023b], outlined in Algorithm 3 and Algorithm 4, respectively. Note that Byz-Clip-SGD is a
variant of DP-SGD in which a robust aggregation rule replaces server-side averaging. Since DP-SGD
is state-of-the-art for private learning, this modification (Algorithm 3) serves as our first baseline.
The method of Allouah et al. [2023b] originally performs per-example clipping (line 5); to align
with our analysis under σ-sub-Gaussian noise, i.e., without assuming a finite-sum structure for fi ,
we instead clip the stochastic gradient.
In experiments of Section 6, we use a coupling of Nearest Neighbor Mixing (NNM) [Allouah et al.,
2023a] and Coordinate Median (CM) [Chen et al., 2017] as an aggregation rule, while Gaussian
mechanism utilizes δ = 4 · 10−4 .

F.2

Label Flipping Attack

We evaluate Byz-Clip21-SGD2M, Byz-Clip-SGD, and Safe-DSHB on CNN and MLP models under
a label-flipping attack [Paudice et al., 2018]. MNIST is evenly partitioned across n = 25 clients;
Byzantine clients flip labels as y → 9 − y. We vary the number of Byzantine clients and the
privacy budget ε ∈ {3, 8, 13, 18, 23}, fixing δ = 4 · 10−4 . Hyperparameter tuning follows Section 6.
All methods use mini-batch gradients with a batch size 32 and no privacy amplification by subsampling. We use a coupling of NNM and CM as a server-side aggregation rule. All algorithms are
run for 60 epochs.

Results in Figure F.1 (bottom line) show that with 1 or 5 Byzantine clients, the methods are
broadly competitive when training MLP model, with Safe-DSHB slightly leading at ε = 0.1. With

the number of Byzantine clients is large.
Experiments on CIFAR10 [Krizhevsky, 2009] with the CNN model showed no significant difference between Byz-Clip21-SGD2M+ and the baselines; therefore, we do not report these results.
While we do not provide convergence guarantees for Byz-Clip21-SGD2M+, our experiments show
that it delivers competitive performance against other baselines when amplification by sub-sampling
is enabled. A theoretical analysis is deferred to future work since the example-wise clipping makes
the analysis substantially more involved than for Byz-Clip21-SGD2M.
